# Character Power Curve

## Core Principle

Progression must be visible, desired, priced, and recontextualized. A protagonist may become overwhelming in one arena if another arena becomes more dangerous: public status, resources, obligations, enemies, law, debt, relationships, secrets, morality, or the size of the stage.

Track four curves separately:

- Capability: combat, skills, knowledge, craft, strategy, negotiation, capital deployment, performance, production, survival.
- Status: rank, fame, fear, trust, official authority, noble legitimacy, guild position, media value, social misunderstanding.
- Resources: money, artifacts, allies, territory, information, shares, titles, facilities, recipes, scripts, IP, contracts.
- Burden: debt, injury, enemies, promises, suspicion, cooldowns, moral cost, public scrutiny, prophecy, scandal, family obligation.

Tension survives when at least one curve resists, lags behind, or rises as a cost whenever another curve improves.

## Default Workflow

When designing or auditing a protagonist growth curve:

1. Identify the genre engine, protagonist origin, current episode range, promised fantasy, and main conflict arena.
2. Classify the protagonist into 1 primary type and 1-2 secondary modifiers.
3. Select a growth curve style: burst, staircase, hidden overpowered, compound accumulation, delayed reward, social ascent, costly exchange, regression optimization, reputation reversal, or authority expansion.
4. Define position emphasis: how the protagonist is seen in the world, not only what they can do.
5. Build the reward structure across capability, status, resources, and burden.
6. Set the next ceiling before the current ceiling breaks.
7. Add crisis intensity through counterplay, social consequence, scarcity, taboo, legal pressure, rival adaptation, or unknown variables.
8. Return a diagnosis plus design tables unless the user asks for prose only.

If the user gives limited context, assume a Korean serialized webnovel with fast early satisfaction, visible 2-3 episode rewards, arc rewards every 10-15 episodes, and a price or complication within 1-3 episodes after a major win.

Prioritize reader-facing serial utility over abstract theory:

- State the protagonist type in one sentence before the table.
- Make the first 5-15 episode application concrete.
- Separate "reader reward" from "in-world reward".
- Mark which reward should be public, hidden, delayed, or paid with a cost.
- If tension is weak, repair the pressure source before adding a stronger enemy.
- If the protagonist is overpowered, move conflict toward control, exposure, collateral damage, law, reputation, resource bottlenecks, or relationships.

## Position Emphasis

Position emphasis is the protagonist's dramatic location in the world. Choose one dominant lens:

- Hidden variable: strong but underestimated, good for early catharsis and reveal timing.
- Public threat: strong enough that institutions move first, good for political or guild pressure.
- Defective genius: brilliant but socially, physically, legally, or morally constrained.
- Illegitimate heir: capable but blocked by bloodline, title, class, sect, family, or contract.
- Market disruptor: wealth or production changes who can attack them.
- Narrative anomaly: regression, possession, system, prophecy, or meta-knowledge makes them dangerous to the world's rules.
- Survival outsider: weak official position, high reader anxiety, strong micro-reward demand.
- Crowned asset: everyone wants to own, recruit, marry, sponsor, expose, or weaponize them.

Every major reward should either change this position or make the current position more dangerous.

## Reward Timing

Use a reward rhythm:

- Micro rewards: clue, minor money, skill hint, enemy embarrassment, fan reaction, small ranking bump, secret leverage.
- Episode rewards: visible win, item, ally, public reaction, new option, contract, survival extension.
- Arc rewards: rank jump, faction shift, major asset, revenge step, territory, secret truth, official appointment, debut stage.
- Season rewards: identity transformation, world-board expansion, major enemy defeat, new ceiling, public myth, irreversible authority.

After a major reward, introduce a price within 1-3 episodes so growth does not flatten tension.

## Rival And Institution Design

Create rivals with one advantage the protagonist cannot simply overpower:

- better legitimacy, status, team, information, public image, backing, law, contract, market access, sponsor, family name, moral leverage, or a counter to the protagonist's signature tool.

Rivals should learn from defeats. If they do not adapt, replace them, promote them into a social obstacle, or change their function into a warning sign for the next ceiling.

## Audit Workflow

When auditing a draft or outline:

1. Map current capability, status, resources, and burden by arc.
2. Mark each reward and its cost.
3. Identify power jumps without setup or consequence.
4. Identify repeated rewards that no longer create desire.
5. Check whether enemies and institutions adapt.
6. Check whether the protagonist's world-position changes after major wins.
7. Add new ceilings, prices, counters, public reactions, or social complications.

If the user provides a broken or vague outline, first name the failure mode, then give the smallest repair that preserves the user's premise.

## Quality Gates

- Every major reward has a cost, counter, or social consequence.
- At least one curve worsens, lags, or becomes more visible when another improves.
- The protagonist's signature advantage remains central.
- Enemies and institutions adapt after learning.
- Rank names, money, cooldowns, injuries, contracts, relationships, and assets remain trackable.
- The next ceiling is visible before the current ceiling is broken.
- Position emphasis changes at least once per major arc.
- Rewards vary across capability, status, resources, and burden instead of repeating only power increases.
- If giving a full design, include a brief self-check or risk note when it would help the user revise the outline.
